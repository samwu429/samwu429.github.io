## 4. Notation and methods to keep exactly

Use the course's symbols and methods; never switch to another textbook's convention.

- One variable (Modules 3–4): observations y₁ … yₙ, sample size n (population size N), sample mean ȳ (x̄ also appears), population mean μ; sample variance S² = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²/(n − 1) and sample standard deviation S = √S² (capital S on the slides; divide by n − 1 "because we estimated the mean"); population σ², σ. Range = Max − Min.
- Median: sort first; rank position 0.5 × (n + 1); for even n average the two middle values. "For skewed distributions, we report the median." The mean is not resistant; the median and IQR are resistant.
- Quartiles by hand: Q1 = median of the lower half, Q3 = median of the upper half (n = 30: 8th value of each half; Tim Hortons donuts Q1 = 210, Q3 = 280, IQR = 70). The slides show only an even-n example. R (`summary()`, `favstats()`) uses another rule (donuts Q1 = 210, Q3 = 277.5, IQR = 67.5) and the slides say: "Do not worry that your hand-based calculation differs from the computer output. We will use R to obtain quartile values." IQR = Q3 − Q1.
- Fences: inner Q1 − 1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR (outside = suspect outlier); outer Q1 − 3 × IQR and Q3 + 3 × IQR (outside = highly suspect outlier). Whiskers go to the most extreme values inside the inner fences; values outside are plotted individually. Outliers are investigated, never simply removed.
- z-score: z = (y − ȳ)/S (population (y − μ)/σ); reverse y = μ + z·σ. For bell-shaped symmetric data only: |z| > 3 highly suspect outlier, 2 < |z| ≤ 3 suspect outlier. Compare values from different distributions by z (larger z = better relative position). z-scores have mean 0 and SD 1 and keep the shape.
- Shifting every value by ± c moves mean, median, quartiles, min, max by ± c and leaves range, IQR, SD unchanged; multiplying by c multiplies centre, position and spread measures by c.
- Empirical Rule (bell-shaped data): about 68% / 95% / almost all within ȳ ± 1S / 2S / 3S; to check it, count the values strictly between the bounds. When the data are not bell-shaped the share can differ (2025 midterm Q17: 62%, not 68%).
- Histograms: equal-width bins, usually 5–30; R's `hist()` bins are right-closed (a, b]. Wording to inequality: "at most 200" ≤ 200, "more than 300" > 300, "at least 300" ≥ 300 (needs raw data when a bin edge splits it). Shape words: unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, uniform, symmetric, skewed to the right / left (named by the longer tail); mean > median suggests right skew but always confirm with the graph.
- Categorical data (Module 2): frequency, relative frequency (proportion between 0 and 1), percentage = proportion × 100. In a contingency table, for a cell: row % = cell / row total, column % = cell / column total, % of overall total (joint) = cell / grand total; marginal distribution = totals / grand total; conditional distribution of the response within each level of the explanatory variable. Association: compare conditional distributions (same → no association / independent). Difference of proportions in **percentage points** (never "% higher"); ratio of proportions with the larger on top ("2.48 times as likely"; between 1 and 2 → "(ratio − 1) × 100% higher"; below 1 → "(1 − ratio) × 100% lower"); no association means difference ≈ 0 and ratio ≈ 1. Simpson's paradox and lurking variables.
- Two variables (Module 5): explanatory (predictor) x on the x-axis, response y on the y-axis. Describe a scatterplot by direction, form, strength (and unusual features). Covariance S_xy = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)/(n − 1); SS_xx, SS_yy, SS_xy; r = S_xy/(s_x s_y) = SS_xy/√(SS_xx SS_yy) = Σ z_x z_y/(n − 1); ρ = population correlation, r = sample estimate; −1 ≤ r ≤ 1, no units, unchanged by changing units or swapping x and y, measures LINEAR association only. Strength scale: strong |r| ≥ 0.8; moderate to strong 0.5 ≤ |r| < 0.8; moderate 0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5; weak |r| < 0.3. Interpretation in SD units: x one SD above its mean → y predicted r SDs above (or below) its mean. Association is not causation (name a lurking variable).
- Regression: ŷ = b0 + b1x (e.g. ŷ = 13.31 + 0.69x); b1 = r·s_y/s_x, b0 = ȳ − b1·x̄ (mark as 补充（课件外） unless the practice solutions use them); residual e = y − ŷ = observed − predicted (negative → overestimated, positive → underestimated); R² = r² (never negative); the intercept is only meaningful when x = 0 is within the data; predicting outside the observed x range is extrapolation; a residual plot with no pattern supports linearity and constant variance.
- Rounding as the course does: proportions to 2–4 decimals, percentages to 1–2 decimals, z to 2 decimals, r to 3–4 decimals; keep full precision until the final answer.

### Interpretation sentences the instructor uses (teach them as `exam` blocks)

- Mean: "On average, <cases> have <mean> <units>."
- Median: "The median <variable> is M. This means that 50% of <cases> have at most M (M or less), whereas 50% have more than M."
- SD: "We can expect <variable> to differ from the mean by about S, on average."
- Distribution: shape, centre, spread and any outliers, with context and units.
- Scatterplot: "There appears to be a <strength> <positive/negative> linear association between <y> and <x>." / "As <x> increases, <y> tends to increase/decrease."
- r in SD units: "If <case>'s <x> was 1 standard deviation above the mean, their <y> is estimated to be |r| standard deviation above/below the mean, on average."
- Slope: "With every additional 1-<unit> increase in <x>, the mean <y> is estimated to increase/decrease by |b1|." (2025 midterm: "When the score in spelling competence increases by 1, the mean score in writing ability is estimated to increase by 0.69.")
- R²: "About <100·R²>% of variation in <y> is explained by the linear regression with <x>."
- Residual: "Residual = observed value − predicted value = y − ŷ."
