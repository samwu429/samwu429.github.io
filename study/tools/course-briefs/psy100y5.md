### The lecture-review sections

They prepare the short-answer half. Walk the deck slide by slide (group slides that belong together), give each lecture definition in an `exam` block, add a `discuss` block with an exam-quality English model answer for every point that could be asked as a short answer, and point back to the textbook section that teaches the topic in depth. Where the lecture and the textbook differ, say so plainly in a `note` (tone `warn`) and teach both: the lecture's version for the short answers, the textbook's for the multiple choice. Known differences:

- The lecture writes UCS and UCR; the textbook writes US and UR (CS and CR are the same in both).
- The lecture lists four primary tastes (sweet, sour, bitter, salty); the textbook adds umami as a fifth.
- The lecture defines sensation as "the stimulation of sense organs" and perception as "the selection, organization, and interpretation of sensory input"; the textbook words both differently.
- The lecture gives three functions of attention, five views of dreaming (psychoanalytic, cognitive, biological, evolutionary threat simulation, problem solving) and lists the difficulties of punishment; check every such list against the textbook.
- REM brain waves: the textbook says REM shows fast waves similar to those of relaxed wakefulness; the lecture says beta waves, and the textbook's own Quiz Yourself key (Chapter 5, LO2-2) also answers beta. Teach beta.
- Each chapter's Quiz Yourself answer key is printed at the end of the chapter ("LO2-2 a"); use it to settle what the textbook counts as correct.
- Gate-control theory, Gustav Fechner and cognitive maps do not appear in this edition's chapters or decks; mention them only as 补充（课件外）.

## 4. Terms and notation to keep exactly

- Use the textbook's wording of every key term (each chapter ends with a Key Terms list; teach every one). English in the station uses the textbook's Canadian spelling: behaviour, colour, centre, judgment as printed.
- Classical conditioning: unconditioned stimulus (US), unconditioned response (UR), neutral stimulus, conditioned stimulus (CS), conditioned response (CR); mention once per section that the lecture writes UCS and UCR.
- Operant conditioning: "positive" = a stimulus is **added**, "negative" = a stimulus is **removed**; reinforcement makes a behaviour **more** likely, punishment makes it **less** likely. Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Schedules: fixed-ratio (FR), variable-ratio (VR), fixed-interval (FI), variable-interval (VI).
- Sleep: stages N1, N2 and N3 (non-REM) and REM sleep; brain waves beta, alpha, theta, sleep spindles, delta — as the textbook names them.
- Drugs: depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens (with the textbook's grouping of each drug).
- Weber's law: two stimuli must differ by a constant proportion (not a constant amount) to be noticed as different. Use only the Weber fractions the textbook gives; any other number is marked `补充（课件外）`.
