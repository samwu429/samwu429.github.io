# Course brief — PHL103H5 F (Fall 2026), Introduction to Philosophy: Knowledge and Reality

University of Toronto Mississauga, Department of Philosophy. Instructor Logan Fletcher. Lectures Monday and Wednesday 1:00–2:00 PM (IB 120) plus a required weekly tutorial. Site `topphi.com/phl103h5`, pack `study/courses/phl103h5`. Read `study/tools/content-spec.md` and `study/tools/review-spec.md` first; this brief adds what is specific to PHL103H5.

The course outcomes (syllabus): understanding and engaging with primary philosophical texts; **extracting and summarizing arguments** from philosophical writing; explaining and offering critical perspective on core ideas, arguments and discussions in metaphysics and epistemology; articulating philosophical content clearly in writing. Teach to these: every reading is taught as questions, theses and **arguments in standard form** (numbered premises, then the conclusion), with the examples that support them and the objections that test them.

### How a reading is taught

1. **背景 (Context)** — who wrote it, the one question the reading answers, why anyone would care (`level` `core`; logistics-free).
2. **论证 (The argument)** — the author's argument in standard form in an `exam` block (English, `P1`, `P2`, …, `C`, each with a Chinese gloss) or a `flow` block for a chain of steps; then one small block per premise saying what it means, why the author believes it, and which example supports it. Use the author's own examples, step by step.
3. **例子与思想实验 (Examples and thought experiments)** — the table, the cat, the billiard balls, the bread, the emeralds, the bat, gradual uploading… each as an `example` block that ends with what it is meant to show.
4. **反驳与回应 (Objections and replies)** — the objections the author raises and answers, and the natural ones a student could raise; `discuss` blocks with a model English answer of 2–5 sentences.
5. **易错点** (`pitfall`) and at least one `check` per section.
6. Where the two readings of a week talk to each other (or a reading talks to an earlier one: Descartes' doubt, Plato's recollection), say so briefly.

`exam` blocks hold the sentences the learner can write on a test: a thesis in one sentence, an argument in standard form, a definition, an objection and its reply.

## 5. Terms and notation to keep exactly

- Arguments: **premise**, **conclusion**, **standard form** (P1, P2, …, C); **valid** = if the premises are true the conclusion must be true; **sound** = valid with true premises. Only call an argument valid or sound where that is clearly right.
- Use each reading's own technical words, with its spelling: Russell's **sense-data**, **sensation**, **physical object**, **appearance** and **reality**, **matter**, **idealism**; Hume's **relations of ideas** and **matters of fact**, **cause and effect**, **a priori**, **experience**, **demonstrative** and **moral** (probable) reasoning, **custom or habit** (the words "induction" and "uniformity of nature" never occur in the Hume file: Hume speaks of the future resembling the past and of the course of nature changing; use the reading's wording and give "problem of induction" and "uniformity of nature" as the standard labels, the first being the syllabus title of the week); Goodman's **grue**, **bleen**, **lawlike** vs **accidental** generalizations, **confirmation** (the excerpt never uses "projectible", "projection" or "entrenchment" and offers no solution: mention them only in a `补充（读物外）` note, and never build a question on them); Nagel's **subjective character of experience**, "what it is like", **physicalism**, **reduction**, **point of view**; Chalmers' **uploading** (destructive, gradual, nondestructive), **functionalism**, **biological** views, **personal identity**; and the terms van Inwagen and Chalmers define in their papers as the notes list them.
- Weeks 2–5 use the translators' words: Grube's **piety** / **the pious**, **the form** (Euthyphro 6d-e; Meno 72c, informal there), **human wisdom**, **gadfly**, **divine sign**, **virtue**, **recollection**, **true opinion**, **knowledge** (true opinion tied down by an account of the reason why), **hypothesis**; Cottingham's **malicious demon**, **I am, I exist**, **a thing that thinks**, the **wax**; Hart's **wonder**, **ontological** vs **aetiological**, **contingency**, **beings** vs **being**; Russell's **indirect** value, the **instinctive man**, **Self** and **not-Self**, **citizens of the universe**. Standard labels that the translations never use (cogito, evil demon / evil genius, method of doubt as a name, dualism, substance, Euthyphro dilemma, divine command theory, elenchus, aporia, daimonion, Meno's paradox as a label) may be taught as the usual names, marked once as `补充（读物外）`, never presented as quotations.
- Two different problems of induction: Hume's (why trust any inference from the observed to the unobserved?) and Goodman's new riddle (which predicates or hypotheses do observations confirm?). Never blur them.

## 6. Questions

- The format is not announced, so each week mixes all three types. Per week aim for **45–60 questions**: **26–34 `mcq` with exactly 4 options**, 4–6 `tf`, 2–3 `multi`, **8–10 `short`** (define and illustrate; state an argument's premises; say what an example shows; give an objection and a reply) and **3–4 `long`** = short essays (explain an argument and assess it; compare two readings; apply a view to a new case). Short-essay `modelAnswer.en` 140–180 words: thesis, the argument in the author's steps, one objection, a reply or assessment.
- Cover every section at least twice and every named example, thought experiment and technical term. About 30% of the `mcq` should be application questions (which of Hume's two kinds of knowledge is this statement; which uploading scenario is this; is this predicate grue-like; which premise does this objection attack); the rest test theses, premises, examples and terms.
- Write original questions; never copy study-guide or test-bank items you may remember.
- Distractors come from real confusions (relations of ideas vs matters of fact, Hume's problem vs Goodman's riddle, sense-data vs physical objects, appearance vs reality, Russell's view vs idealism, subjective vs objective in Nagel's sense, physicalism vs functionalism, gradual vs destructive uploading, the author's view vs a view the author attacks). Every explanation says why the right option is right and why each tempting wrong one is wrong.
- Spread the correct answers evenly over A–D: options are shown in the order written.
